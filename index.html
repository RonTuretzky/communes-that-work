Session 4

Contemporary Communes That Work

The commune is not a failed 1960s experiment. It is a living organizational form with century-long track records, a measurable contemporary footprint, and a documented history of state and media campaigns against it. This session covers the form, the campaign, and the communities running today.

Slide 2 · The argument

Three claims, in order

1. The form works

Income-sharing communities exist today with 59-year (Twin Oaks), 117-year (kibbutz), and 498-year (Hutterite) track records. The question was never whether communal living is viable — it is which structures make it durable.

2. The reputation was partly manufactured

Declassified FBI and CIA documents show deliberate state campaigns to infiltrate, discredit, and stigmatize communes — including a 1970 FBI directive ordering informant coverage of every New Left commune in America.

3. The opening is now

Housing costs, care deficits, and loneliness are the exact problems communal economics solve. The contemporary wave — ecovillages, cohousing, community land trusts — is already the largest since the 1970s.

Roadmap: define the form (slides 3–5) → what scale would threaten (6) → the campaign against it, in the documents' own words (7–8) → why the 60s wave actually receded (9) → the arithmetic of scale (10) → the communities working today, on four continents (11–16).

Slide 3 · Definitions

What a commune is — and the two adjacent forms

Three nested terms get conflated. The distinctions matter because critics attack the broadest form using the narrowest form's stereotypes — and because the legal system treats them very differently.

Commune

narrowest · income-sharing
  • Income and assets pooled. Members work for the community; the community meets needs (housing, food, healthcare, allowance).
  • Common purse is the defining test — not shared meals or shared land.
  • Living examples: Twin Oaks & the FEC network, Hutterite colonies, Bruderhof, shitufi kibbutzim, Yamagishi jikkenchi (Japan), Nomadelfia (Italy).
  • Rarest form — and the one with the longest unbroken track records.

Intentional community

the umbrella term
  • People who choose to live together around shared values, with some degree of shared resources and governance.
  • Includes communes, but also cohousing, housing co-ops, ecovillages, land trusts, monasteries, ashrams.
  • FIC estimates 3,500+ in the US, 10,000–30,000 worldwide; 1,200+ listed in the ic.org directory.
  • Most share some things (land, meals, decisions) without pooling income.

Ecovillage

the ecological wing
  • An intentional or traditional community designed for ecological + social regeneration (GEN definition).
  • Term coined 1991 (Gilman report for Gaia Trust); network formalized at Findhorn 1995.
  • GEN connects 6,000+ communities in 114 countries (2025), incl. traditional villages adopting the framework.
  • Footprint evidence: Findhorn residents ≈ half UK average; Sieben Linden CO₂ ≈ ⅓ German average.
The slur "commune" was used in 1970s zoning fights against all three forms. In Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas (1974) the Supreme Court upheld ordinances that excluded any household of more than two unrelated people — communal or not. Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas, 416 U.S. 1 (1974) — justia.com

Slide 4 · The wider model

Intentional community — the working examples

The umbrella form is not hypothetical. Each of these is operating today; every name links to the community or its directory entry.

Twin Oaks

Virginia · 1967 · ~100 people · income-sharing

The longest-running secular income-sharing community in the US. Full deep-dive on slide 12.

The Farm

Tennessee · 1971 · ~200 people

Founded by 300 San Francisco caravanners; survived a 1983 debt crisis by restructuring from full commune to dues-based cooperative. Home of the modern midwifery revival (Ina May Gaskin).

East Wind

Missouri · 1974 · 1,045 acres · income-sharing

Twin Oaks' first spinoff. Runs East Wind Nut Butters (~$500k/yr) and Utopian Rope Sandals.

Bruderhof

1920 · ~3,000 members · 20+ communities, 4 continents

Anabaptist full community of goods; runs Community Playthings (classroom furniture) as its economic engine. 105 years of unbroken common purse.

Hutterite colonies

1528 · 544 colonies · ~58,400 people · growing

The longest continuous communal economy on Earth. Colonies split amoeba-like at ~120 people — a tested protocol for scaling by division.

Ganas

Staten Island, NYC · 1979 · ~75 people

Urban intentional community across eight adjacent houses; core group income-shares, wider circle shares housing and daily feedback meetings. Proof the form doesn't require land.

Adjacent forms with the same DNA: cohousing (~170+ US communities; private homes + common house), student housing co-ops, and community land trusts (slide 16).

Slide 5 · The ecological wing

Ecovillage — where the term comes from, what it means

1991: Gaia Trust — the Danish foundation of Ross and Hildur Jackson — commissions Robert and Diane Gilman (Context Institute, Seattle) to survey sustainable communities. Their report, Ecovillages and Sustainable Communities, coins the modern term and definition. 1995: ~400 people gather at Findhorn, Scotland for the "Ecovillages and Sustainable Communities for the 21st Century" conference; the Global Ecovillage Network (GEN) is founded there. Today: GEN reports 6,000+ connected communities across 114 countries; 1,295 formally mapped, 252 joined in 2024 alone.

"A human-scale, full-featured settlement in which human activities are harmlessly integrated into the natural world in a way that is supportive of healthy human development, and can be successfully continued into the indefinite future." — Robert Gilman's 1991 definition, Context Institute

GEN's four dimensions of regeneration — the framework every member community designs against:

🌱 Ecological

Food, water, energy, building, waste. Measured results: Findhorn footprint 2.71 gha/person ≈ half the UK average (UHI/SEI 2006, lowest recorded in the industrialized world); Sieben Linden CO₂ under ⅓ of German average (Univ. of Kassel); Dancing Rabbit targets ~10% of US consumption.

🤝 Social

Decision-making, conflict process, inclusion, education, care. The dimension that kills communities when skipped — governance and membership process, not composting, is where most failures happen (see slide 9).

💰 Economic

Right livelihood on the land: community businesses, local currencies (Damanhur's credito), shared infrastructure that drops cost of living — the practical answer to "how do you afford it."

🌍 Cultural / worldview

Shared story, celebration, purpose. The Sosis & Bressler data (slide 9) shows shared worldview + costly commitments is the single strongest longevity predictor across 83 communes.

Slide 6 · Stakes

What communes-at-scale would dismantle

Before looking at how communes were attacked, be clear about what a mass communal sector would actually threaten. None of this is speculative — each mechanism is observable in existing communities; scale is the only missing variable.

The rent relation

Income-sharing communities hold land and housing collectively — no landlord, no mortgage per household. Twin Oaks houses ~100 people on a budget that would cover perhaps a dozen Bay Area rents. A communal sector at scale is a structural withdrawal of tenants from the rental market and buyers from the speculative housing market.

Labor discipline via the exit option

The threat behind every bad job is "you need this paycheck." A credible, decent communal alternative functions like a strike fund that never runs out — it raises the wage floor and bargaining power of everyone, the same way kibbutzim shaped Israeli labor norms for decades.

The care market

Childcare, eldercare, cooking, and sick care are internalized as valued labor (Twin Oaks counts them at full labor-credit parity with business work). At scale this pulls billions of dollars of demand out of paid care markets — and reprices "women's work" everywhere by example.

Atomized consumption

One commune kitchen replaces 25 kitchens; one tool shed replaces 25 garages of duplicate equipment. The measured result is the Findhorn/Sieben Linden footprint data — consumption per person collapses without lifestyle collapse. Multiply by millions and entire consumer-goods categories shrink.

A movement that withdraws rent, labor desperation, care spending, and duplicate consumption — simultaneously — is not a lifestyle quirk. That is why the surveillance files in the next two slides exist.

Slide 8 · The campaign, mechanized

The discredit campaign — tactic by tactic

Four documented tactics, each with its process and its payoff, from the Church Committee record. These are findings of a US Senate investigation, not allegations.

① Define & index — criminalize the living arrangement

Define "commune" as inherently Marxist-revolutionary (4/17/70 directive)
→
Investigate every member of every commune matching it
→
Add members to the Security Index (detention list)
Payoff: shared income itself becomes probable cause. No act required — the household structure is the offense.
"A rebuttable presumption exists that persons having a past history of participation in violent leftist radical activity… living in a communal relationship constitutes a commune within the above definition."Book III p. 517

② Infiltrate — flood the form with informants

Huston Plan lowers informant age limit to 18 (Sept 1970)
→
SAC Letter 70-48: "aggressive policy of developing new productive informants"
→
Target list: "collectives, communes and staffs of their underground newspapers"
Payoff: chronic internal distrust. Every new member is a possible plant — poisoning the open-door culture communes depended on.
"…a tremendous opportunity to expand your coverage."SAC Letter 70-48, 15 Sept 1970, Book III p. 533

Sends it to Huey Newton attacking Eldridge Cleaver

Slide 9 · The honest autopsy

Why the wave receded — the real reasons

Timothy Miller documented 1,600+ verified communes from 1960–75 and argues the true number ran to the tens of thousands, involving hundreds of thousands of people. Most dissolved within years. The defeatist reading — "communes don't work" — does not survive contact with the evidence. The wave ran a massive natural experiment under uniquely hostile conditions, and the variables that separate survivors from casualties are now well documented.

The headwinds (external)

  • Law: Belle Terre v. Boraas (1974) — Supreme Court upholds zoning that bans more than two unrelated people per home. Health-code raids and occupancy limits were standard anti-commune tools; FIC still calls land-use regulation the most pernicious obstacle to community formation.
  • No legal vessel: nothing between "single-family home" and "apartment building" fit; no entity type held collective equity, so assets dissipated with each departure.
  • Violence and surveillance: in Taos County (≈27 communes at peak) communes faced arson, dynamite, beatings, a murder — on top of the federal program on slides 7–8.
  • Macro timing: the 1973–75 recession ended cheap rural land; the draft's end (1973) removed a push factor.

The unforced errors (internal — and fixable)

  • Open doors: "everyone welcome" admission with no selection or labor accountability is the most-cited failure mode — working families left when their labor subsidized transients.
  • Undercapitalization: marginal land, no equity structure, donation income, farm inexperience.
  • No governance: charisma or anarchy instead of decision process; Sosis's data shows the top recorded dissolution causes were internal dispute (n=48) and economic failure (n=43) — solvable collective-action problems, not laws of nature.
The longevity evidence: Sosis & Bressler (2003), studying 83 nineteenth-century US communes: median lifespan ~25 years for religious vs ~5 for secular — but once the number of costly commitments (resource-sharing rules, dress codes, food norms) is controlled for, "religion" stops mattering. Kanter (1972) found durable 19th-century communities deliberately engineered commitment (sacrifice, investment, communion…). The 60s survivors still alive — Twin Oaks (1967), Padanaram (1966), The Farm (1971), East Wind (1974), Sandhill (1974) — share exactly that profile: income-sharing, member selection, real governance, binding culture. Design, not luck.

Slide 11 · Infrastructure

Foundation for Intentional Community — the movement's commons

Before looking at individual communities, meet the institution that connects them. The FIC is the seventy-seven-year-old backbone organization of the North American communities movement — its directory is how people find communities, and its publications are how communities learn from each other's failures.

1948 → 1986 → 2019

Founded as the Fellowship of Intentional Communities in 1948 — itself an outgrowth of inter-community cooperation among 1930s–40s communities. Reincorporated 1986 at a meeting at Stelle, Illinois. Renamed Foundation for Intentional Community in 2019.

The Directory

ic.org/directory lists 1,200+ communities, searched by 500,000+ people a year — the de-facto matchmaking layer between seekers and communities. FIC estimates 3,500+ US communities total (most never list publicly).

Communities magazine

Published since 1972 (first as Communitas), 200+ issues of practitioner knowledge: governance, conflict, money, zoning. Publishing transferred to GEN-US in 2019; archive at ic.org.

Why it matters for the thesis: a movement with a directory, a magazine, a legal-resource library, and annual gatherings is not a scattering of experiments — it is an institutionalized sector that has been accumulating organizational memory since 1948. Twin Oaks (next slide) does paid admin work for the FIC: the communities literally staff their own infrastructure.

Slide 12 · Case study

Twin Oaks — income-sharing, 59 years in

Founded 1967 by eight people with no farming experience on a 123-acre Virginia tobacco farm, inspired by B.F. Skinner's Walden Two and co-founder Kat Kinkade. Today: ~450 acres, ~85 adult members + 15 children, full income sharing. "My other car isn't mine either."

The labor-credit system

Every member owes 38.5 hours/week (down from 47 in the early years) — and all work counts equally: milking, cooking, childcare, gardening, and business work earn identical credit. Less than half of all hours go to income-generating work; the rest is the domestic economy that ordinary households do unpaid and uncounted.

The businesses

Hammocks were 75–80% of income in the "Pier 1 decades" (70s–90s); when Pier 1 dropped them, income recovered within two years by diversification — today: tofu/soyfoods (~30% of income, sold to Whole Foods among others), book indexing, seed growing for Southern Exposure Seed Exchange, contract admin work for the FIC, and Twin Oaks Textiles (the hammock shop burned in 2024; a rope-hammock line continues with East Wind).

Healthcare

The community pays all basic medical and dental care, overseen by an internal Health Team — plus catastrophic coverage through PEACH (right).

The spinoff lineage — communes that reproduce

  • East Wind (1974, Missouri): founded when Twin Oaks hit capacity — Kinkade went on to co-found it. 1,045 acres; nut butters & rope sandals.
  • Acorn (1993, Virginia): founded by Twin Oakers (incl. Kinkade) 7 miles away; runs Southern Exposure Seed Exchange, a major heirloom/organic seed company.
  • Sapling (2013): Acorn bought land to seed a third community; it folded, and Mimosa took over the land — even the failures get recycled into new attempts.
  • Cambia (2015): founded by ex-Twin Oakers next door; tiny-house village in the Twin Oaks visitor orbit.

The FEC — federation since 1976

The Federation of Egalitarian Communities (first assembly at East Wind, Dec 1976; Kinkade modeled it on kibbutz federations) links Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Sandhill + a dozen communities in dialog. Two concrete mutual-aid systems:

  • PEACH — the federation's self-insurance fund for catastrophic health costs: the home community covers roughly the first $5,000 of a claim; PEACH pays the rest. Communal healthcare, federated.
  • LEX (labor exchange) — work at any FEC community and earn labor credits at home, with travel subsidized. A common labor market across communes: Sandhill imports harvest help each fall and sends members out each winter.

Slide 13 · The contemporary wave

Ecovillages — the largest commune wave since the 70s

Findhorn · Scotland · 1962

~400 residents at The Park · ~40 community businesses

Started by the Caddys and Dorothy Maclean in a caravan park; became the movement's mother church — GEN was founded here in 1995. The 2006 UHI/SEI study measured residents' footprint at 2.71 gha — half the UK average, the lowest recorded in the industrialized world. The Foundation closed its education arm in 2023; the ecovillage itself continues under community ownership.

Auroville · India · 1968

~3,300 residents from ~60 nations · UNESCO-endorsed

The largest intentional community on Earth, founded by Mirra Alfassa near Puducherry, endorsed by four UNESCO resolutions, centered on the Matrimandir. No private land ownership; an internal economy with a maintenance system instead of wages for many roles. Since 2021 in a governance struggle with the centrally-appointed Auroville Foundation.

Tamera · Portugal · 1995

~150–200 residents · 335 acres, Alentejo

"Healing Biotope 1" — peace research center known for its water-retention landscape that re-greened a desertifying valley, its solar village test field, and decades of research on community, trust, and love free of possession.

Crystal Waters · Australia · 1987

~250 residents · 259 ha · UN World Habitat Award 1996

The world's first intentional permaculture village — 83 freehold lots on land 80% held in common, designed by Mollison-lineage permaculturists. Proof the model works with private homes + commons hybrid tenure.

EcoVillage at Ithaca · NY · 1991

200+ residents · 3 cohousing neighborhoods · 175 acres

One of the world's largest cohousing complexes: Frog, Song, and Tree neighborhoods, ~100 homes, 90% of land kept open, newest homes net-zero. The mainstream-friendly face of the wave — driveways, mortgages, and a common house.

Sieben Linden · Germany · 1997

~140 people · world's densest straw-bale settlement

Univ. of Kassel research: per-capita CO₂ under one-third the German average, housing emissions down ~90%. ~75% vegetable self-sufficiency. Subject of a book-length Routledge environmental-impact study (open access).

Dancing Rabbit (Missouri, 1997, ~50–70 people) deserves mention for the hardest covenants in the US: no personal cars, no fossil fuel for heating or cooling, organic land use only — targeting ~10% of average US consumption.

Slide 14 · Europe's densest scene

Italian communes — where the practice actually lives

Italy hosts one of Europe's deepest communal cultures — Catholic, esoteric, and anarchist lineages running in parallel, several with full common purses.

Nomadelfia · Tuscany · 1948

~250–300 people · Catholic · zero internal money

Founded by priest Don Zeno Saltini around families who adopted war orphans. No money circulates inside; all property common; children raised by "family groups" of several households. Recognized within the Catholic Church; Pope Francis visited in 2018. Seventy-seven years of common purse — the longest-running full commune in Western Europe.

Damanhur · Piedmont · 1975

~600 citizens in nucleo-households · own currency

Esoteric federation in the Valchiusella valley founded by Oberto Airaudi: ~20-person nucleo communities, an internal complementary currency (the credito), elected guides, schools, and dozens of businesses. Famous for the Temples of Humankind — a cathedral-scale underground complex the community excavated in secret for 14 years before its 1992 discovery. GEN member, frequently cited as Europe's largest spiritual ecovillage federation.

Urupia · Puglia · 1995

anarchist · consensus · common purse

Libertarian commune on a restored masseria in Salento: full income pooling, consensus-only decisions, wine and olive-oil production marketed through Italy's solidarity-economy networks. The living refutation of "anarchist communes can't run a business."

Popolo degli Elfi · Tuscan Apennines · ~1980

~200 people across reoccupied mountain hamlets

The "Elf People" reoccupied abandoned villages above Pistoia, restoring stone houses and chestnut-terrace agriculture: subsistence farming, no formal hierarchy, seasonal assemblies. Italy's depopulated mountains — thousands of empty hamlets — are the land bank this model demonstrates.

Context: Italy's Rete Italiana Villaggi Ecologici (RIVE) federates ~30+ communities; the abandoned-village stock (estimated in the thousands) keeps land cost near zero for new projects — the structural opposite of the US land problem on slide 9.

Slide 15 · Beyond the West

Asia — the oldest living lineages

The commune is not a Western counterculture artifact. Asia hosts the world's largest intentional community, Japan's century-old service communes, Thailand's Buddhist merit-economy villages, and the kibbutz — the most successful communal movement in modern history, currently at an all-time population high.

Yamagishi-kai · Japan · 1950s

~1,200 members · income-sharing jikkenchi villages

Founded by farmer Miyozo Yamagishi; members live without money or personal possessions in jikkenchi ("places to realize the principles"). Toyosato (Mie Pref.) is the largest with 400+ members; eggs and dairy at industrial scale. Affiliates in Australia, Brazil, Thailand, Korea, Switzerland.

Konohana Family · Japan · 1994

~100 members at the foot of Mt. Fuji

One common bank account pays for everything from clothes to university tuition; nightly consensus meetings; grows ~99% of its own food (200+ crop varieties); footprint ≈ ⅓ the Japanese average.

Santi Asoke · Thailand · 1975

~10 self-sufficient Buddhist villages · ~10,000 adherents

Founded by Samana Bodhirak after splitting from the official Sangha. Bunniyom ("meritism") economy — goods sold at or below cost to earn merit, not profit. Organic farms, own schools, free meals. Its model prefigured Thailand's official "sufficiency economy" doctrine.

Kibbutzim · Israel · 1909

~270 kibbutzim · ~182,000 residents — all-time high

From Degania (1910, still extant) to today: after a 1990s crisis, ~72% adopted differential salaries ("renewed"), while ~60 remain fully income-sharing — yet all retain mutual guarantee floors, and population has grown every year since the mid-2000s. Adaptation, not collapse.

Sarvodaya Shramadana · Sri Lanka · 1958

~15,000 villages · ~5,500 independent village societies

A.T. Ariyaratne's Gandhian-Buddhist movement: village self-governance through shared labor (shramadana), cooperative economics, microfinance. Not communes — but the largest communal-governance network in Asia.

Ittōen · Kyoto · 1904

~100–150 residents · 121 years old

Tenkō Nishida's community of "the life of the Light": members renounce possessions and practice humble service (famously, cleaning toilets as spiritual practice). Own school and businesses. Older than every Western commune now operating except the Hutterite/Bruderhof lineage.

cross-refAuroville (slide 13) is also Asia's largest intentional community — ~3,300 residents in Tamil Nadu.

Reading the lenses

Full communes (Twin Oaks, Hutterites, Nomadelfia) score 4–5 on every lens. The hybrids each take one or two lenses to maximum inside the existing state: Christiania and CLTs maximize common land; Mondragón maximizes governance at firm scale; Marinaleda does it as a municipality.

Strategic read: the lenses are separable. You can decommodify land without sharing income, or share governance without sharing meals — and each separable piece has already been proven somewhere at scale.
